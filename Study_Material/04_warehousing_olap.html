🏛️ Data Warehouse • Card 1 of 25
💼 What IS a Data Warehouse? — The Analogy

Imagine two parts of a business:

🏪 The cash register — It handles transactions right now. Customer buys something → register records it instantly. This is your operational database (OLTP).

📒 The accountant's annual report binder — It doesn't process today's sales. Instead, it accumulates years of data — revenues, trends, comparisons — for the CEO to study patterns. This is your data warehouse.

The key insight: the warehouse is NOT for day-to-day transactions. It's for stepping back and asking big questions like "How did our sales grow over the last 5 years?"

💡 Why it matters: Without a warehouse, every "how are we doing?" question would slow down the live transaction system — like forcing the cashier to stop and write a quarterly report mid-sale!
🏛️ Data Warehouse • Card 2 of 25
📋 Inmon's 4 Key Properties

W.H. Inmon (the "father of data warehousing") defined a data warehouse with 4 properties:

1. 🎯 Subject-Oriented
Organized around subjects (Sales, Inventory) — not departments (HR, Finance). Like sorting a library by topic, not by who donated the book.
2. 🔗 Integrated
Data from many source systems is cleaned and merged. One unified definition for "customer" or "product" across all departments.
3. ⏳ Time-Variant
Keeps historical snapshots. An OLTP system shows the current price; the warehouse shows the price on every date in history.
4. 🔒 Non-Volatile
Data is loaded in bulk, then read-only. Nobody updates individual rows. Old data stays unchanged — it's a historical record.
🏛️ Data Warehouse • Card 3 of 25
⚡ OLTP vs OLAP — Two Worlds

OLTP Online Transaction Processing — the engine of daily business.

It handles fast reads and writes: "Did customer #123 pay today?" "Update stock level for product #456." Thousands of small operations per second.

OLAP Online Analytical Processing — the engine of business intelligence.

It handles complex, slow queries over massive data: "What was the total revenue by region in Q3 2025 vs Q3 2024, broken down by product category?"

🚀 Think of OLTP as a surgeon performing an operation (precise, fast, live) and OLAP as a medical researcher studying patterns across thousands of patient records (thorough, analytical, historical).

🎲 Data Cube • Card 6 of 25
🎲 Data Cube — Pivot Table on Steroids

You've probably used an Excel pivot table — rows are months, columns are products, cells show sales. That's a 2D view.

Now imagine a 3D cube where the three axes are:

📦 Product 📅 Time 🌍 Region

Each tiny cell inside the cube holds a number — like total sales for that Product × Time × Region combination.

💡 The magic of a data cube is that you can instantly sum or aggregate along any axis — giving you quick answers to multi-dimensional questions.
🎲 Data Cube • Card 7 of 25
📐 Fact Table + Dimension Tables

A data cube is implemented as two types of tables in a database:

📊
Fact Table

The numbers you measure: Sales Amount, Revenue, Quantity Sold, Profit. These are your metrics.

Also holds foreign keys pointing to dimension tables.

📋
Dimension Tables

The context for those numbers: Products (name, category), Dates (day, month, year, quarter), Customers, Locations.

These are your axes of the cube.

🍕 Pizza analogy: Fact table = the receipt (amounts). Dimension tables = the menu (what product), calendar (what day), address book (which location).

📏 Measure Types — Distributive, Algebraic, Holistic

Not all aggregation functions (measures) work the same way:

✅ Distributive — Can compute from sub-parts.
Total sales = North sales + South sales + East sales. Examples: COUNT, SUM, MIN, MAX
🔧 Algebraic — Formula of distributive pieces.
AVG = SUM / COUNT. Both SUM and COUNT are distributive, so AVG can be computed. Examples: AVG, STANDARD_DEVIATION
❌ Holistic — Need ALL data together.
You CANNOT combine medians of regions to get overall median. Must see all values. Examples: MEDIAN, MODE, RANK
⚠️ Holistic measures are expensive in data warehouses — they can't be pre-computed in parts!

Can you compute the OVERALL MEDIAN sales by combining the median of each region? Why or why not?
✅
NO! Median is holistic. You need ALL values sorted together.

Example: Region A: [1,2,3] → median=2. Region B: [10,20,30] → median=20.
Combined [1,2,3,10,20,30] → median = (3+10)/2 = 6.5. Not 2 or 20!

🗺️ OLAP Operations • Card 15 of 25
🔼 Roll-up — Climbing Up the Hierarchy

Roll-up reduces detail by climbing up a concept hierarchy or reducing dimensions.

Jan 1st sales → January sales → Q1 sales → 2025 sales

You can also roll up by reducing a dimension — aggregate across all regions to get a global total.

SQL equivalent: GROUP BY year instead of GROUP BY month.

💡 Memory hook: Roll-up = Rolling up a map to see less detail but a bigger area. "Zoom out."
🗺️ OLAP Operations • Card 16 of 25
🔽 Drill-down — Into the Details

Drill-down is the reverse of roll-up. You navigate from summary to detail.

2025 sales → Q1 sales → Jan sales → Jan 1 sales

Real example: You see Q1 revenue dropped. You drill-down to see which month caused it, then which week, then which day. You're a detective finding the culprit! 🕵️

💡 Drill-down can also mean adding a new dimension — going from "sales by region" to "sales by region AND product category."
🗺️ OLAP Operations • Card 17 of 25
✂️ Slice & 🎛️ Dice
✂️ Slice

Fix ONE dimension to a single value.

3D cube → 2D plane

Example:
"Show me only Q1 data"
Time = 'Q1'
🎛️ Dice

Fix MULTIPLE dimensions to specific ranges.

3D cube → smaller 3D sub-cube

Example:
"Q1, Electronics, North"
Time='Q1' AND Cat='Elec'
🧲 Easy memory trick: Slice = cheese slicer (removes one layer). Dice = cutting food into cubes (cuts in multiple directions at once).

🏗️ Schema Types • Card 19 of 25
⭐ Star Schema

The simplest warehouse schema. One central fact table, surrounded by denormalized dimension tables.

✅ Pros: Simple queries, fast performance.

⚠️ Cons: Dimension tables may have redundant data (denormalized).

💡 A star schema is like a solar system — one sun (fact table) with planets (dims) orbiting it directly.
🏗️ Schema Types • Card 20 of 25
❄️ Snowflake Schema

Like a star schema, but dimension tables are normalized — split into sub-tables to remove redundancy.

✅ Pros: Less storage (no redundancy), cleaner data.

⚠️ Cons: More complex JOIN queries, slower performance.

💡 Snowflake = star with icy branches! The dim tables grow outward into sub-tables.
🏗️ Schema Types • Card 21 of 25
🌌 Fact Constellation (Galaxy Schema)

When you have multiple fact tables that share some dimension tables, you get a Fact Constellation — also called a Galaxy Schema.

Use case: One company tracks both Sales and Orders, sharing the same Time and Location dimensions.

🌳 AOI • Card 22 of 25
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 AOI — Summarizing Your Friends

Imagine you want to describe your friend group to someone new. You could say:

❌ "Alice: 22, works at Google, New York, $80k. Bob: 25, works at Meta, Seattle, $90k. Carol: 23, works at Amazon, Seattle, $85k..."

OR you could say:

✅ "Most friends are young professionals working at Big Tech companies in the Pacific Northwest/New York area with good salaries."

That's Attribute-Oriented Induction (AOI) — automatically generalizing specific data into a readable summary by climbing concept hierarchies.

💡 AOI helps you go from a messy database table to a clean, human-readable summary — automatically!
🌳 AOI • Card 23 of 25
🔁 AOI — Step by Step

AOI works in a series of steps:

1
Start with a data relation — your initial query result (e.g., all student records).
2
Remove irrelevant attributes — e.g., drop Student_ID (too specific, not useful for patterns).
3
Generalize attribute values — Replace "New York" with "Northeast USA", or "22" with "Young Adult".
4
Merge duplicate rows — After generalization, many rows become identical; merge and count them.
5
Stop when table is small enough — A few rows that summarize the whole dataset.

📘 SQL GROUP BY Refresher

GROUP BY aggregates rows sharing a common value:

SELECT region, SUM(sales) AS total
FROM sales_table
GROUP BY region;

This is the SQL equivalent of a Roll-up. Adding more columns to GROUP BY = more granular cuboid.

🔷 Jim Gray's CUBE Operator

SQL GROUP BY CUBE(a, b, c) computes ALL possible GROUP BY combinations — the full lattice of cuboids.

SELECT product, time, location, SUM(sales)
FROM sales
GROUP BY CUBE(product, time, location);

For 3 dimensions, this runs 2³ = 8 queries automatically! ROLLUP(a, b, c) only computes the hierarchy path: abc → ab → a → ().

⚡ ROLAP vs MOLAP vs HOLAP

ROLAP — Relational OLAP

Stores data in regular relational tables. Flexible but slower for complex queries.

MOLAP — Multidimensional OLAP

Stores data in pre-built multidimensional arrays. Very fast but requires more storage and less flexible.

HOLAP — Hybrid OLAP

Combines both: detailed data in relational storage, aggregates in multidimensional arrays. Best of both worlds.

🔢 Bitmap Index Basics

A bitmap index represents each distinct value as a bit-vector (0s and 1s).

Example — "Region" column with values North, South, East:

Row  | North | South | East
-----|-------|-------|-----
  1  |   1   |   0   |   0    ← Row 1 is North
  2  |   0   |   1   |   0    ← Row 2 is South
  3  |   0   |   0   |   1    ← Row 3 is East
  4  |   1   |   0   |   0    ← Row 4 is North

To find all North rows: just read the "North" column (mostly 0s and 1s — super fast!). Bitmap indexes are ideal for low-cardinality warehouse dimensions.
