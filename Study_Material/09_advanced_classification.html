Section 1: Feature Engineering • Card 1 of 30

Why Feature Selection? The Real-World Dilemma

In classical introductory machine learning, we make a massive, unrealistic assumption: "A clean training table with $n$ rows and $p$ perfectly chosen attributes is already handed to us."

Reality in Data Analytics: In enterprise datasets, we often collect thousands or tens of thousands of features (gene expression levels, browser cookies, sensor telemetry, text tokens). Most of these features are noisy, redundant, or completely irrelevant to our target variable.

🎯 The Triple Curse of Too Many Features:

  • Overfitting: Algorithms memorize spurious correlations in high dimensions instead of real patterns.
  • Curse of Dimensionality: In high dimensions, points become equidistant and distance-based metrics (k-NN, clustering) break down.
  • Computational Cost: Training time explodes exponentially or quadratically with $p$.

Feature Selection Goal: Select a minimal subset of $k$ features ($k \ll p$) that maximizes classification accuracy while improving model interpretability.

Section 1: Feature Engineering • Card 2 of 30

The 3 Pillars: Filter, Wrapper & Embedded Methods

1. Filter Methods

Model Independent

Ranks features purely using statistical properties ($\chi^2$ test, correlation, mutual information). Fast ($O(p)$), but ignores interactions between features.

2. Wrapper Methods

Model as Evaluator

Uses the classifier itself to test feature subsets (Forward Selection, Backward Elimination). Very accurate, but computationally expensive ($2^p - 1$ subsets).

3. Embedded Methods

Built-in Selection

Feature selection happens simultaneously during model training. E.g., Decision Tree node splits, LASSO ($L_1$ penalty forcing zero coefficients).

Slide Rule of Thumb: When you have $100,000$ features, run a fast Filter first to drop to $500$, then use a Wrapper or Embedded model for final optimization!

Section 2: Bayesian Networks • Card 5 of 30

Breaking the "Naive" Assumption: Bayesian Belief Networks

Recall from Module 08 that Naive Bayes makes a radical assumption: Class Conditional Independence:

$$P(\mathbf{X} \mid C) = \prod_{k=1}^d P(x_k \mid C)$$

In medical diagnosis, this assumption fails catastrophically! A patient having a cough and a fever are strongly dependent because both are caused by a common viral infection. Treating them as independent counts the same evidence twice!

Bayesian Belief Networks (BBN): A graphical model that specifies which subsets of variables are conditionally independent, allowing us to model rich causal interactions between variables.

Section 2: Bayesian Networks • Card 6 of 30

The Two Components of a Belief Network

A Bayesian Belief Network is formally defined by two mathematical components:

  1. Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG): Nodes represent random variables (features, disease, symptoms). Directed arcs represent direct causal influences ($A \to B$ means $A$ has a direct effect on $B$).
  2. Conditional Probability Tables (CPT): For every node $Y$, a table specifies the conditional distribution $P(Y \mid \text{Parents}(Y))$.
Joint Probability: $P(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(x_i \mid \text{Parents}(X_i))$

If a node has no parents, its CPT is just its prior probability $P(X)$. This factorization radically reduces the number of probabilities required from $2^n$ to a small polynomial!

Section 2: Bayesian Networks • Card 8 of 30

Training Scenarios & Markov Chains

How Do We Train a Bayesian Network? (Slide #20)

Scenario 1: Structure Known + All Variables Observable
Straightforward! Simply count empirical frequencies in training tuples to fill CPT entries (just like Naive Bayes).
Scenario 2: Structure Known + Hidden Variables Present
Use Gradient Descent (hill-climbing) or the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to iteratively infer latent variables.

🔗 Markov Chains (Slide #21):

A special memoryless stochastic process: the probability of the next event depends only on the current state, not historical states: $P(X_{t+1} \mid X_t, \dots, X_1) = P(X_{t+1} \mid X_t)$. Common example: Google Gmail Smart Compose autocomplete!

Section 3: Support Vector Machines • Card 9 of 30

Support Vector Machines: The Maximum Margin Principle

Pioneered by Vladimir Vapnik in 1992, SVM is one of the most mathematically rigorous and robust classification algorithms in computer science.

Imagine you have two classes (circles and squares) separated by a blank corridor. Many lines could separate them. Which one is best?

The Core Philosophy: Pick the hyperplane that leaves the largest possible safety margin on both sides! A decision boundary that hugs training points closely will misclassify slightly noisy new test points. A wide margin maximizes generalization.

⭐ Support Vectors: Only the training points lying directly on the margin boundaries determine the decision boundary! If you remove all other non-boundary points from the training set, the decision boundary does not change at all!
Section 3: Support Vector Machines • Card 10 of 30

Mathematical Formulation of the MMH

For linearly separable data where labels $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$:

Any separating hyperplane is written as: $$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b = 0$$

The two bounding margin planes are:

$$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b \ge +1 \quad (\text{for } y_i = +1)$$

$$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b \le -1 \quad (\text{for } y_i = -1)$$

Margin Width = $\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$

To maximize the margin $\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$, we must minimize $\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ subject to $y_i(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$. This is solved using Lagrange multipliers ($\alpha_i$) via quadratic programming.

Section 3: Support Vector Machines • Card 11 of 30

Non-Linear SVM & The "Kernel Trick"

What if the data is linearly inseparable in 2D (e.g., inner circle of red dots surrounded by outer ring of blue dots)?

The Genius Insight: Project the data into a higher-dimensional space ($\Phi(\mathbf{x})$)! In 3D (e.g., adding $z = x^2 + y^2$), the inner circle pops up into a bowl, and a flat 2D plane can slice cleanly beneath it!

🪄 The Kernel Trick ($K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$):

We never actually need to calculate the expensive coordinates in high-dimensional space! We only need the dot product $\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i) \cdot \Phi(\mathbf{x}_j)$, which can be computed directly in low dimensions using a Kernel function:

  • • Polynomial Kernel: $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{z} + 1)^d$
  • • RBF (Radial Basis Function / Gaussian): $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|^2)$

Section 4: Rule-Based Classification • Card 14 of 30

Rule-Based Classifiers: IF-THEN Logic

Instead of continuous formulas, a rule-based classifier represents knowledge as a collection of human-readable IF condition THEN conclusion rules:

R1: IF age = youth AND student = yes THEN buys_computer = yes
R2: IF credit_rating = excellent THEN buys_computer = no

Two Key Metrics for Rule Evaluation (Slide #38):

  • Coverage: Fraction of tuples that satisfy the IF condition. $\text{Coverage} = \frac{n_{\text{covers}}}{|D|}$.
  • Accuracy: Fraction of covered tuples where the rule's prediction matches the true label. $\text{Accuracy} = \frac{n_{\text{correct}}}{n_{\text{covers}}}$.
Section 4: Rule Induction • Card 15 of 30

Sequential Covering Algorithm (FOIL, RIPPER)

How do we learn rules directly from raw training data without human programming?

The "Separate-and-Conquer" Strategy (Slide #41):

1 Learn-One-Rule: Start with an empty condition and greedily add attribute predicates that maximize rule purity.
2 Filter / Remove: Remove all positive training tuples covered by this newly learned rule from dataset $D$.
3 Repeat: Iterate until remaining tuples belong to the majority/default negative class.

Widely implemented in algorithms like FOIL (First-Order Inductive Learner) and RIPPER (Repeated Incremental Pruning to Produce Error Reduction).

Section 4: Associative Mining • Card 16 of 30

Associative Classification & CBA (KDD 1998)

Why build decision trees or sequential rules when we already have powerful frequent pattern mining algorithms like Apriori and FP-Growth?

Associative Classification: Mines association rules where the consequent (right-hand side) is restricted strictly to the class label:

$p_1 \wedge p_2 \wedge \dots \wedge p_k \longrightarrow \text{Class} = C_i$

CBA (Classification Based on Associations - Liu, Hsu, Ma 1998):

  1. Mine all frequent association rules with minimum support and confidence.
  2. Sort rules by confidence (higher confidence first), then support, then shorter rule length.
  3. Greedily select the top rules that correctly classify training instances, discarding redundant rules.
Section 5: Weak Supervision • Card 17 of 30

Weak Supervision: The Data Labeling Crisis (Slide #48)

So far, algorithms (Decision Trees, SVM, Naive Bayes) assumed Strong Supervision: thousands of pristine, expert-annotated labels.

The Reality Bottleneck:
  • Annotating 1 minute of human speech requires 10 minutes of human labor.
  • Annotating environmental audio sounds takes 400 times longer than real time!
  • Medical pathology image annotation requires certified oncologists ($200+/hr).

Modern machine learning must classify data using Weak Supervision: partial, noisy, or zero training labels!

Section 5: Weak Supervision • Card 18 of 30

Semi-Supervised Learning & Self-Training

Suppose you have 100 labeled tuples and 100,000 unlabeled tuples.

Self-Training Procedure (Slide #49):

1. Train an initial classifier on the 100 labeled tuples.
2. Use the classifier to predict labels for all 100,000 unlabeled tuples.
3. Select the tuples with the highest confidence scores (e.g. probability $\ge 0.98$).
4. Add these pseudo-labeled tuples to the training set.
5. Retrain the model and repeat until convergence!

⚠️ Risk (Error Propagation): If early pseudo-labels are incorrect, errors compound with each iteration. Fix with consensus voting or co-training.

Section 5: Weak Supervision • Card 19 of 30

Active Learning: Asking the Oracle Smart Questions

Instead of passively accepting random labels, the Active Learner actively queries a human expert (an "Oracle") only for the hardest, most ambiguous examples (Slide #51).

Uncertainty Sampling:

If the model predicts an image is 99% a dog, querying the human wastes money. But if the model is 50.1% cat vs 49.9% dog, querying the human provides maximum information gain!

Result: Active learning frequently reaches the same accuracy as full supervised learning using only 10% to 20% of the labeling budget!

Section 5: Weak Supervision • Card 20 of 30

Transfer Learning & TrAdaBoost (Slide #52)

Traditional ML: Starts from scratch for every task. A classifier trained on camera reviews knows nothing about TV reviews.

Transfer Learning: Extracts features and representations learned from a rich Source Task and applies them to a data-sparse Target Task.

TrAdaBoost (Transfer AdaBoost - Slide #53):

When source and target data share the same feature space but differ in distribution, TrAdaBoost re-weights source samples. It lowers weights of source samples that conflict with target data while boosting helpful ones!

Section 5: Weak Supervision • Card 21 of 30

Zero-Shot Learning: Classifying Unseen Classes

The Ultimate Challenge: Can an algorithm classify an object into a class it has never seen a single training example of? YES, through Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL)!

Slide #56 Semantic Attribute Paradigm:

We define high-level semantic attributes: [4_legs, wings, claws, night_vision].

  • Known training class: Dog $= [1, 0, 0, 0]$
  • Known training class: Owl $= [0, 1, 0, 1]$
  • Known training class: Fish $= [0, 0, 0, 0]$

During testing, a new image appears. The semantic attribute predictor outputs: $[1, 0, 1, 1]$. Even though the classifier never saw a Cat during training, the knowledge base defines Cat as $[1, 0, 1, 1]$. The model predicts "Cat" with zero training images!

Section 6: Rich Data Types • Card 23 of 30

Classification with Rich Data Types (Slide #59)

🌊 Stream Data

Continuous arrival (credit card transactions). Impossible to store in memory. Must classify in one single pass and handle concept drift (VFDT / Hoeffding trees).

🧬 Sequence Data

Ordered values (DNA codons: A, C, G, T; sentence text). Predict sequence properties like sentiment or coding regions using sequence kernels or LSTMs.

🕸️ Graph Data

Node-level: Classify web pages or social users. Graph-level: Classify chemical molecules (toxic vs non-toxic) using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).

Section 6: Advanced Paradigms • Card 24 of 30

Multiclass Classification: OVA & ECOC Codes

Binary classifiers (like basic SVM) only distinguish between 2 classes. How do we classify $m$ classes ($m > 2$)?

1. One-vs-All (OVA / One-vs-Rest): Train $m$ binary classifiers. Classifier $j$ treats class $j$ as positive and all other $m-1$ classes as negative. Predict with the classifier outputting highest confidence.
2. Error-Correcting Output Codes (ECOC - Slide #67): Borrowed from telecommunications! Assign each class a unique $n$-bit binary codeword. Train $n$ independent binary classifiers (one per bit). Even if 1 or 2 classifiers predict wrong bits, Hamming distance error-correction correctly recovers the true class!
Section 6: Interpretability • Card 25 of 30

Model Interpretability & LIME (Slide #68)

In high-stakes applications (medicine, loan approvals, criminal justice), high accuracy is not enough: we must know WHY a model made a decision.

🍋 LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations):

Even if a global deep network or SVM is wildly complex, in the tiny neighborhood right around a single prediction, the decision boundary looks almost flat (linear)!

LIME perturbs the input, sees how predictions change, and fits a simple linear surrogate model locally to explain which features contributed positively or negatively to that specific decision.

Section 6: Soft Computing • Card 26 of 30

Rough Sets: Lower & Upper Approximations (Slide #73)

Introduced by Zdzisław Pawlak, Rough Set Theory handles uncertainty caused by imperfect information where two different instances have identical feature values but different class labels.

Lower Approximation $\underline{R}(C)$:

The set of objects that are definitely and unambiguously members of class $C$. (Zero doubt).

Upper Approximation $\overline{R}(C)$:

The set of objects that possibly belong to class $C$.

Boundary Region = $\overline{R}(C) - \underline{R}(C)$ (The region of vagueness).

Section 6: Soft Computing • Card 27 of 30

Fuzzy Sets & Membership Functions (Slides #74–87)

Classical Logic (Crisp): An element belongs to a set ($1$) or it does not ($0$). If "Young" is defined as $\le 30$, a person on their 30th birthday is young, but on their 30th birthday + 1 day they are old!

Fuzzy Logic (Lotfi Zadeh): Objects have a degree of membership $\mu_A(x) \in [0.0, 1.0]$.

Example (Slide #87):
  • Age 25: $\mu_{\text{Young}} = 1.0, \mu_{\text{MiddleAged}} = 0.0$
  • Age 35: $\mu_{\text{Young}} = 0.4, \mu_{\text{MiddleAged}} = 0.6$ (Transition state)
  • Age 55: $\mu_{\text{Young}} = 0.0, \mu_{\text{MiddleAged}} = 0.8, \mu_{\text{Senior}} = 0.2$
