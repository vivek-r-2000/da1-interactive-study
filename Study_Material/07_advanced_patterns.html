🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 1 of 7
📰 The Newspaper Ad Analogy

Imagine you're a newspaper publisher selling banner ad slots. An advertiser wants their ad seen by as many different visitors as possible.

They don't care if the same visitor sees it 100 times — they want broad COVERAGE across unique visitors.

Coverage patterns find the best combination of web pages to place banners for maximum reach — even if those pages don't all have high individual traffic.

🤔 Why does this matter?
Standard frequent patterns track how often items appear TOGETHER. Coverage patterns track how many UNIQUE users are reached by AT LEAST ONE item in a set. Completely different goal!
🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 2 of 7
🔄 Coverage Set vs Frequent Set

Frequent patterns: How often do users visit these pages together (same session)?

Coverage patterns: How many unique users visit AT LEAST ONE of these pages?

MetricFrequent MiningCoverage Mining

💡 Key insight: Supersets in coverage patterns can INCREASE coverage (more pages → more unique users reached), so Apriori's pruning doesn't directly apply.
🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 3 of 7
📐 Key Terms: RF, CS, OR

Three numbers define whether a set of pages is a good coverage pattern:

📏 Relative Frequency (RF): Fraction of sessions where a page appears.
RF(page) = sessions with page / total sessions
📏 Coverage Support (CS): Fraction of users who visit AT LEAST ONE page in the pattern.
CS(pattern) = |union of users across pages| / total users
📏 Overlap Ratio (OR): How much pages share their audience.
Low OR = diverse audience → great for ad coverage!

🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 5 of 7
⚠️ Why Standard Frequent Mining Fails Here

Coverage support does NOT have the downward closure (anti-monotonic) property.

A pair of pages might have HIGH coverage even if individual pages are infrequent.

Example: Page A reaches users 1-100. Page B reaches users 101-200. Neither is frequent individually, but together they cover 200 unique users — high coverage!

Apriori would prune {A, B} because A and B are individually infrequent. But that's the WRONG call for coverage mining.
💡 Consequence: We need a NEW algorithm that exploits different properties to avoid exponential search.
🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 6 of 7
⚙️ CMine Algorithm

CMine is the key algorithm for coverage pattern mining. It exploits the sorted closure property.

  1. Sort items by decreasing frequency
  2. Level-wise search (like Apriori in structure)
  3. Prune using Non-Overlap Patterns (NOP)
  4. A pattern is a NOP if its overlap ratio is ≤ maxOR
  5. Sorted closure: if a pattern is NOP, its super-patterns (in sorted order) are also NOP
🌟 Insight: By sorting items in decreasing frequency order, we can prune huge parts of the search space when a pattern exceeds the overlap threshold.
🌐 Coverage Patterns • Card 7 of 7 — Active Recall
🃏 Flip Card: CMine Sorting

❓ Why does the CMine algorithm sort items in DECREASING order of frequency?

The sorted closure property: if a pattern has low overlap (is a non-overlap pattern), then adding items in frequency order ensures sub-patterns are also non-overlap. This enables level-wise pruning to skip huge branches of the search tree. 🌳✂️

🏪 Multi-Level Rules • Card 1 of 6
🛒 The Grocery Store Zoom-Out Analogy

Imagine finding patterns in grocery purchases. If you look at very specific items — "Foremost 2% Milk" — the support is tiny. Almost nobody buys the exact same brand.

But zoom out to "milk" and patterns are obvious: milk → bread appears constantly!

Multi-level mining lets you find rules at different levels of specificity simultaneously.

🤔 Why does this matter?
Real product databases have hierarchies: Brand A 2% Milk → Milk → Dairy → Food. Patterns at one level might not exist at another. We need mining that crosses these levels.
🏪 Multi-Level Rules • Card 2 of 6
⚠️ The Specificity Problem

At the specific item level, support is too low to be meaningful. At the category level, support is rich. We want to find rules at multiple abstraction levels simultaneously.

Using a single min_support threshold for all levels forces a painful trade-off: too high → miss specific rules; too low → flood of trivial general rules.

🏪 Multi-Level Rules • Card 4 of 6
📐 Ascending Minimum Support

The clever solution: use DIFFERENT min_support values at different levels.

Rule: Higher min_support at low-specificity levels (broad categories appear often).
Lower min_support at high-specificity levels (specific items are rarer).

min_support(L1) > min_support(L2) > min_support(L3)
🌟 Effect: This balances discovery across levels. You find meaningful patterns whether looking at broad categories or specific products without flooding the output.
🏪 Multi-Level Rules • Card 5 of 6
🗑️ Redundancy Filtering

Multi-level mining can produce rules that are nearly identical across levels. We filter these as redundant.

Example:
L2 rule: milk → bread, confidence = 60%
L3 rule: 2% milk → bread, confidence = 61%

The L3 rule adds only 1% more information! It's redundant. Why report both?

A rule at a specific level is redundant if its ancestor rule at a higher level has similar confidence. We use a redundancy ratio threshold to filter such cases.

💡 Outcome: Cleaner, more interpretable output without duplicating essentially the same insight at multiple levels.
🏪 Multi-Level Rules • Card 6 of 6 — Active Recall
🃏 Flip Card: Deeper = Lower Threshold

❓ Why do we need LOWER min_support at deeper (more specific) levels of a concept hierarchy?

Specific items appear in fewer transactions. "Brand A 2% Milk" is bought by far fewer people than "milk" in general. If we applied the same high threshold, we'd find NO patterns at the specific level — even meaningful ones would be filtered out. 🔍

📊 Quantitative Rules • Card 1 of 5
🎯 The "Age Range Buys Sports Cars" Analogy

Standard association rules work with yes/no items: bought milk? Yes or no.

But what about: "People aged 25–35 with income $50k–$80k tend to buy sports cars"?

This involves RANGES of numbers — quantitative attributes. We need to convert these ranges to yes/no first, which creates its own challenges.

🤔 Why does this matter?
Most real-world data has numeric columns (age, income, purchase amount). Ignoring them loses huge amounts of information. Quantitative rules let us find patterns that span continuous attributes.
📊 Quantitative Rules • Card 2 of 5
⚠️ The Discretization Catch-22

To use numeric values in association rules, we discretize them: convert continuous values into bins (intervals).

Too many small intervals:
age=[25–26] appears in very few transactions → LOW support. Patterns get pruned before discovery.

Too few large intervals:
age=[0–100] covers everyone → HIGH support but USELESS. No real pattern captured.
The sweet spot: We need intervals that are neither too narrow (low support) nor too wide (low confidence). This is the core challenge of quantitative rule mining.
📊 Quantitative Rules • Card 3 of 5
🔧 Dynamic Discretization — The Smart Solution

Instead of fixing bins upfront, dynamic discretization finds them from the data:

  1. Start with the smallest possible intervals (each unique value)
  2. Merge adjacent intervals that are "similar" (e.g., close values)
  3. Keep merging until minimum support is met
  4. Stop when further merging would lose too much precision
🌟 Benefit: Data-driven intervals — the bins adapt to where the data actually clusters, rather than using arbitrary cutoffs like "25, 35, 45...". More natural and more likely to find real patterns.
📊 Quantitative Rules • Card 4 of 5
🗂️ Multi-Dimensional Rules
TypeExample
Single-dimbuys(milk) → buys(bread)
Multi-dimage(25–34) AND income(50k–80k) → buys(sports_car)
Hybridage(25–34) AND buys(sports_car) → income(60k–90k)

Multi-dimensional rules span multiple columns of a relational table. They're much richer than single-column rules.

💡 Real use: Customer segmentation, risk scoring, targeted marketing — any task that combines demographic data with behavioral data. This is the foundation of modern data-driven decisions.
📊 Quantitative Rules • Card 5 of 5 — Active Recall
🃏 Flip Card: The Discretization Catch-22

❓ What is the Catch-22 of static discretization for quantitative association rules?

If intervals are too small: low support (few transactions share the same narrow range). If intervals are too large: low confidence (the range covers too many people to be informative). Neither extreme works — and any fixed split is arbitrary! 🎯

💰 High-Utility Mining • Card 1 of 5
🍫 The Truffle vs. Bread Analogy

Frequent itemset mining counts how often items appear together. But what if some items are more valuable?

A store sells 1,000 loaves of cheap bread and 10 luxury truffles. Which generates more revenue?

If truffle profit is $50 each and bread profit is $0.50 each:
Bread total = $500 | Truffles total = $500. Equal revenue, but totally different frequency!

🤔 Why does this matter?
Frequent mining would rank bread patterns far above truffle patterns. But for maximizing revenue, truffle combos are equally important. High-utility mining focuses on VALUE, not just frequency.
💰 High-Utility Mining • Card 2 of 5
📐 Utility = Quantity × Profit
External utility (profit per unit):
Beer = $2 profit, Wine = $5 profit, Cheese = $3 profit

Internal utility (quantity bought in each transaction):
Transaction T1: Beer×3, Wine×1, Cheese×2

Utility of {Beer, Wine} in T1:
= (3×$2) + (1×$5) = $6 + $5 = $11

Total utility of {Beer, Wine}: sum across all transactions where both appear
🌟 Goal: Find all itemsets whose total utility ≥ minUtility threshold.

💰 High-Utility Mining • Card 4 of 5
⚙️ Two-Phase Algorithm

High-utility mining can't use Apriori directly because utility is NOT anti-monotonic. The two-phase approach solves this:

Phase 1 — Candidate Generation:
Use Transaction-Weighted Utilization (TWU) as an OVER-ESTIMATE of utility. TWU IS anti-monotonic! Generate all candidates where TWU ≥ minUtility.
Phase 2 — Exact Computation:
Scan the database once more to compute the EXACT utility of each candidate. Keep only those ≥ minUtility.
TWU(itemset X) = sum of transaction utilities of ALL transactions containing X
(counts the whole transaction value, not just X's contribution → overestimate)
💰 High-Utility Mining • Card 5 of 5 — Active Recall
🃏 Flip Card: Why Can't We Use Apriori?

❓ Why can we NOT directly apply Apriori to high-utility itemset mining?

Utility is NOT anti-monotonic. Adding more items to an itemset can INCREASE its utility (because you're adding more valuable items). So if a subset has low utility, its supersets might still have high utility — we cannot safely prune. Apriori's core assumption is broken! ⚡

🔒 Constraint-Based • Card 1 of 3
🍽️ The Restaurant Search Analogy

Mining ALL patterns in a database is like asking: "Tell me everything interesting about this city." Overwhelming and mostly useless.

Constraint-based mining is like asking: "Find all affordable restaurants within 2km that have veggie options." The constraints guide the search to only relevant patterns.

🤔 Why does this matter?
In practice, users always have domain knowledge. "I only care about items where total price ≤ $100" or "I want patterns involving only electronics." Encoding these as constraints eliminates huge portions of the search space before computation.
🔒 Constraint-Based • Card 2 of 3
📋 Four Types of Constraints
TypeDefinitionExampleAction
Anti-monotonic If violated, all supersets also violate sum(price) ≤ $100 Prune supersets
Monotonic If satisfied, all supersets also satisfy contains(beer) Skip re-checking
Succinct Directly limits candidate items items in Electronics Reduce candidates
Convertible Can become anti/monotonic with ordering avg(price) ≥ $50 Sort then prune
🌟 Strategy: Push anti-monotonic constraints DEEP into the search (prune early). Use monotonic constraints to skip redundant checks. Combine for maximum efficiency.
🔒 Constraint-Based • Card 3 of 3 — Active Recall
🃏 Flip Card: Anti-Monotonic Constraint

❓ Give an example of an anti-monotonic constraint and explain WHY it is anti-monotonic.

sum(price) ≤ $100

If a set of items already exceeds $100, adding MORE items (all with positive prices) can only increase the sum further. So ALL supersets also violate the constraint. We can safely prune the entire subtree! ✂️💸

📐 Advanced Formulas & Concepts
📏 Diversity Rank (drank) Formula
drank(P) measures how diverse a pattern's audience is.
drank(P) = |∪ users(p_i)| / Σ |users(p_i)|

A drank of 1 means NO overlap (perfectly diverse). A drank of 1/|P| means complete overlap (all pages reach the same users).
🔴 Negative Patterns & Null-Invariance
Negative pattern: Items that rarely occur together despite high individual frequency.

Null-invariant measures (e.g., Kulczynski, Cosine) don't change value when you add transactions with neither item. This is important because most transactions don't contain most items.
🏆 Redundancy-Aware Top-k Patterns (MMS)
MMS (Maximum Marginal Significance) selects top-k patterns that are both significant AND diverse.
Each new pattern in the top-k must add marginal value beyond what's already known. Inspired by MMR (Maximal Marginal Relevance) in information retrieval.
📈 Sequential Pattern Mining Overview
Sequential patterns find ordered sequences of events. Example: "customers who buy camera → then buy memory card → then buy carrying case."

Key algorithms: GSP (Generalized Sequential Patterns), PrefixSpan (prefix-projection), SPADE (vertical format).
Support: fraction of sequences containing the pattern (order matters!).
