📚 Data Comes in Many Flavors

Think about how you'd describe students in a class:

🏷️ Name — "Priya" (a label, no math possible)
🔢 Age — 20 years (a number, you can compute average)
🏆 Rank — 3rd in class (ordered, but gaps may vary)
🌡️ Temperature — 37.2°C (equal gaps, but 0°C ≠ no temperature)

Each of these requires different statistical tools. Using the wrong tool is like using a ruler to measure color — it just doesn't make sense!

Data attributes fall into two big families: Qualitative (categories) and Quantitative (numbers). Each family has subtypes.

🎯 Why does this matter? Choosing the wrong attribute type for analysis leads to meaningless results. Knowing your data type tells you exactly which operations and charts are valid.

🏷️ Types of Data & Attributes • Card 3 of 30
🎨 Nominal Data — Categories Without Order

Imagine sorting crayons by color: red, blue, green, yellow. The colors are just labels — there's no "better" or "worse" color, no ranking.

✅ You CAN: Say blue ≠ brown. Count how many people have blue eyes. Find the MODE (most common).
❌ You CANNOT: Say blue > brown. Compute a mean eye color. Subtract colors.

Examples: Eye color, blood type (A/B/O/AB), marital status, country of birth, movie genre.

Special case — Binary: Only 2 values. Yes/No, True/False, Male/Female. Often encoded as 0 and 1 for computation.

Valid operations: = and ≠ only. Mode is the only meaningful central tendency measure.

🎯 Why does this matter? Machine learning algorithms need nominal variables encoded as numbers. But treating "1=Red, 2=Blue, 3=Green" as if 3>2>1 is a serious mistake — it implies an order that doesn't exist!
📊 Types of Data & Attributes • Card 4 of 30
🌟 Ordinal Data — Ordered but Unequal Gaps

Think of a restaurant rating: ⭐, ⭐⭐, ⭐⭐⭐, ⭐⭐⭐⭐, ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐. We know 5★ > 4★ > 3★. But is the gap between 1★ and 2★ the same as between 4★ and 5★? Not necessarily!

✅ You CAN: Rank order the values. Say A > B. Find mode and median.
❌ You CANNOT: Say "4★ is exactly twice as good as 2★." Compute a meaningful mean.

More examples: Academic grades (A/B/C/D/F), Likert scale (Strongly Agree → Strongly Disagree), cancer stages (Stage I, II, III, IV), socioeconomic status (Low, Medium, High).

Valid operations: =, ≠, >, <. Mode and Median are meaningful. Mean is questionable.

🎯 Why does this matter? Survey data (like "rate your satisfaction 1–5") is ordinal. Reporting the average satisfaction score sounds clean, but it can be misleading. Use the median or frequency distribution instead.
🌡️ Types of Data & Attributes • Card 5 of 30
📏 Interval Data — Equal Gaps, No True Zero

Temperature in Celsius is a perfect example. The gap between 20°C and 30°C is exactly the same as between 30°C and 40°C (equal gaps ✅). But: 0°C does NOT mean "no temperature." So we can't say 40°C is "twice as hot" as 20°C.

⚠️ Common mistake: Saying "It's twice as hot today (40°C) as yesterday (20°C)" — this is WRONG for Celsius! Use Kelvin for true ratios.
✅ You CAN: Subtract (30°C − 20°C = 10°). Add. Compute mean & median.
❌ You CANNOT: Say "30°C is 1.5× hotter than 20°C." Compute meaningful ratios.

Examples: Temperature (°C, °F), calendar years, IQ scores, pH levels.

Valid operations: =, ≠, >, <, +, −. Mean, Median, Mode all valid.

🎯 Why does this matter? IQ scores are interval data. An IQ of 140 is NOT "twice as intelligent" as IQ 70. The zero on the IQ scale is arbitrary, not absolute.
⚖️ Types of Data & Attributes • Card 6 of 30
⚖️ Ratio Data — True Zero, Full Math Power

Weight, height, salary, distance — these have a true zero meaning "none of that thing." 0 kg literally means no weight. This means ALL math operations are valid!

✅ You CAN: Say 60 kg is twice 30 kg. Compute ratios. Add, subtract, multiply, divide. Mean, Median, Mode all valid. Also: Geometric mean makes sense!
❌ You CANNOT: Have negative values in most ratio scales (you can't weigh −5 kg).

Examples: Height (cm), weight (kg), salary (₹), distance (km), age, number of children, blood pressure.

Temperature in Kelvin is ratio! 0K truly = no heat (absolute zero). 400K is twice as hot as 200K.

🎯 Why does this matter? Financial data (revenue, profit) is ratio — you can legitimately say "Company A earns 3× more than Company B." This enables powerful comparative analysis.

🌊 What Makes Data "Big"?

Big Data isn't just about how much data there is. Think of a hospital during a pandemic — it faces 5 simultaneous challenges:

📦 Volume
Millions of patients, test results, records
🎭 Variety
Text notes, X-rays, sensor feeds, spreadsheets
⚡ Velocity
Real-time vitals updating every second
🤔 Veracity
Contradictory symptoms, incomplete forms
💎 Value
All of this data can potentially save millions of lives

Regular databases can't handle these 5 challenges together. Big Data technologies (Hadoop, Spark) were built specifically for this.

🎯 Why does this matter? You need to diagnose which "V" is the problem before choosing a Big Data solution. Not all big datasets have all 5 Vs.

📦 Volume — The Scale of Data

Volume refers to the sheer quantity of data generated. This has grown exponentially with smartphones, IoT sensors, social media, and e-commerce.

🌐 Google: Processes ~20 petabytes of data per DAY
📱 WhatsApp: ~100 billion messages sent daily
📷 Instagram: ~100 million photos uploaded per day
Data Scale Reference:
1 KB = 1,000 bytes (a short email)
1 MB = 1,000 KB (a photo)
1 GB = 1,000 MB (a movie)
1 TB = 1,000 GB (a year of HD video)
1 PB = 1,000 TB = 1 million GB
1 EB = 1,000 PB (all internet traffic in a month)

Traditional SQL databases can't hold or query petabyte-scale data efficiently. That's why Google built BigTable and why Hadoop/Spark exist.

🎯 Why does this matter? Volume determines storage architecture. At petabyte scale, you distribute data across thousands of commodity servers rather than one giant expensive machine.

🎭 Variety — Many Formats, One System

Modern data isn't just neat tables in spreadsheets. Data comes in 3 main structures:

📋

🎯 Why does this matter? A single hospital has all three types: structured patient records, semi-structured HL7 messages, unstructured doctor's notes and X-ray images.
⚡ 5 Vs of Big Data • Card 13 of 30
⚡🤔💎 Velocity, Veracity & Value
⚡ Velocity — Speed of data generation and processing
Stock market: millions of trades per second. IoT sensors: readings every millisecond. Credit card fraud detection: must decide in <100ms. Technologies: Kafka, Spark Streaming, Flink.
🤔 Veracity — Trustworthiness and accuracy of data
Social media: fake news, bots, contradictory accounts. Medical records: missing values, entry errors, conflicting diagnoses. "Garbage in, garbage out" — bad data = wrong decisions. Data quality is a massive problem.
💎 Value — Extracting meaningful insights from raw data
Raw data has no value until analyzed. Netflix uses viewing history to save $1B/year in customer retention. Banks use transaction data to detect fraud in real time. Value is the ultimate goal of all data analytics.
🎯 Why does this matter? Many organizations collect lots of data (Volume ✅) but fail to extract Value because they ignored Veracity (bad data quality). Data cleaning can take 60–80% of a data scientist's time!

📈 Statistical Descriptions • Card 15 of 30
🌤️ Statistics = A Weather Forecast for Data

A weather forecast doesn't show you every single atmospheric pressure reading across every square kilometer. It gives you the key summary: 28°C, 70% chance of rain.

Statistics does the same for data. Instead of looking at 10,000 individual rows, you get a few powerful numbers:

🎯 Central Tendency
Where is the "center" of the data? (Mean, Median, Mode)
📐 Spread / Dispersion
How spread out is the data? (Range, Variance, SD)
📉 Shape
Symmetric? Skewed left or right? (Skewness, Kurtosis)
🔗 Relationship
Do two variables move together? (Correlation)

These 4 categories of descriptive statistics give you a complete picture of any dataset.

🎯 Why does this matter? Before building any ML model, data analysts spend hours computing these statistics to understand the data — this is called Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA).
📊 Statistical Descriptions • Card 16 of 30
🎯 Mean, Median & Mode — The Three Centers

The "Bill Gates walks into a bar" effect: Imagine 9 people earning ₹30,000/month average. Bill Gates walks in. Now the mean jumps to millions — but the median barely changes. Which tells the "true" story?

➕
Mean

Sum ÷ Count. Affected by outliers. Best for symmetric data.

⬛
Median

Middle value. Resistant to outliers. Best for skewed data.

👑
Mode

Most frequent value. Works for any data type including nominal!

For data [2, 3, 3, 5, 100]: Mean = 22.6 (distorted by 100). Median = 3 (much more representative!). Mode = 3.

India's per capita income statistics: always check if they're reporting mean or median — they tell very different stories given income inequality.

🎯 Rule of thumb: Skewed data → use Median. Symmetric data → Mean ≈ Median, either works.

📐 Statistical Descriptions • Card 18 of 30
📐 Measures of Spread — How Scattered Is the Data?

Two exam classes, same mean score of 70. But are they really the same?

🅰️ Class A
Scores: 68, 69, 70, 71, 72
Range: 4 | SD ≈ 1.4
Very consistent!
🅱️ Class B
Scores: 20, 35, 70, 95, 100
Range: 80 | SD ≈ 30.4
Wildly unequal!
📏 Range = Max − Min (simple but sensitive to outliers)
📊 Variance = Average of squared deviations from mean (in squared units)
📉 Standard Deviation (SD) = √Variance (back in original units, intuitive!)
📦 IQR = Q3 − Q1 (middle 50% of data, robust to outliers)

SD = 0 means all values are identical. A higher SD = more spread = less consistency.

🎯 Why does this matter? In finance, SD of stock returns = risk! Higher volatility (SD) means higher risk. The Sharpe Ratio uses SD to measure risk-adjusted returns.

🔔 Statistical Descriptions • Card 20 of 30
🔔 The Normal Distribution (Bell Curve)

Most measurements in nature cluster around an average, with fewer and fewer values as you move away. This creates a bell-shaped curve.

🟦 68% of data falls within ±1 SD of mean
🟦🟦 95% of data falls within ±2 SD of mean
🟦🟦🟦 99.7% of data falls within ±3 SD of mean
This is the famous "68-95-99.7 Rule" or Empirical Rule

Examples: Heights of adults, measurement errors, IQ scores, manufacturing tolerances — all approximately normal.

If a score is more than 3 SDs from the mean, it's considered an extreme outlier (probability <0.3%).

🎯 Why does this matter? Many ML algorithms assume normality. If your data isn't normal (skewed, heavy-tailed), you may need to transform it first using log or sqrt transformations.
↗️ Statistical Descriptions • Card 21 of 30
↗️ Skewness — Which Way Does the Tail Point?

When data isn't symmetric, one side has a longer "tail." The direction of the tail defines the skew.

◀
Negative Skew

Tail on LEFT
Mean < Median
e.g., age at retirement

⬆️
Symmetric

No tail
Mean = Median
e.g., heights

▶
Positive Skew

Tail on RIGHT
Mean > Median
e.g., income, housing prices

💡 Income is positively skewed — billionaires like Ambani create a long right tail that pulls the mean far above the median. That's why "median household income" is a fairer measure than "average income."

A quick rule: If Mean > Median → positive skew. If Mean < Median → negative skew.

🎯 Why does this matter? Skewed data violates normality assumptions of many statistical tests. You can fix skew using log transformation (common for income data), square root, or Box-Cox transformation.

⚠️ CORRELATION ≠ CAUSATION!
Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are both higher in summer → they're positively correlated. But eating ice cream doesn't cause drowning! The hidden cause is HOT WEATHER making people swim AND buy ice cream.

This third variable causing both is called a confounding variable or lurking variable.

🎯 Why does this matter? Machine learning features with high correlation can cause multicollinearity issues. In causal analysis, you need randomized experiments to establish causation, not just correlation.

📊 Data Visualization Types • Card 26 of 30
📊 Histograms vs. Bar Charts — Know the Difference!
⚠️ Common confusion! Histograms and bar charts look similar but serve completely different purposes.
📶 Histogram
For continuous/quantitative data
Bars touch each other (no gaps!)
X-axis = bins (ranges)
Shows distribution/frequency
Example: Distribution of student ages in buckets 18-20, 20-22, 22-24...
📊 Bar Chart
For categorical/discrete data
Bars have gaps between them
X-axis = categories
Shows counts per category
Example: Number of students with each hair color (Black, Brown, Blonde...)

The key difference: Histograms show data shape (normal? skewed?). Bar charts compare discrete groups.

You can reorder bars in a bar chart (no problem). You cannot reorder bins in a histogram — they follow the number line!

🎯 Exam tip: When asked "which chart for continuous variable distribution?" → Histogram. "Which for comparing categories?" → Bar chart.
🔵 Data Visualization Types • Card 27 of 30
🔵 Scatter Plots, Parallel Coords & Boxplots
⬤ Scatter Plot
Use: Show relationship between 2 continuous variables
Each point = one data item (x, y). See clusters, outliers, linear/nonlinear relationships. Foundation of regression analysis.
∥ Parallel Coordinates
Use: Visualize multi-dimensional data (many attributes at once)
Each vertical axis = one attribute. Each line = one data item crossing all axes. Great for finding clusters in high-dimensional data (e.g., wine quality with 13 chemical features).
📦 Boxplot (Box-and-Whisker)
Use: Show 5-number summary + outliers, compare groups
Box = IQR (Q1 to Q3). Line inside = Median. Whiskers = ±1.5×IQR. Points beyond whiskers = outliers. Perfect for comparing distributions across categories.
🎯 Why does this matter? Parallel coordinates are used in industrial quality control to monitor many sensors simultaneously. One "out of pattern" line instantly reveals a faulty machine.

📚 Additional Slide Topics & Exam Foundations

📋 Primary vs. Secondary Data & 12 Survey Question Types

• Primary Data: First-hand raw data gathered directly by the investigator through surveys, interviews, observations, focus groups, or experiments.

• Secondary Data: Data collected earlier by someone else for another purpose (e.g., census records, tax/electoral data, hospital archives, third-party reports).

• Quantitative vs Qualitative Methods: Quantitative analyzes numerical measurements and frequencies. Qualitative collects deep subjective insights (one-on-one interviews, case studies).

• 12 Survey Question Types (from slides): (1) Single-answer multiple choice, (2) Rating scales, (3) Likert scale (Agree/Disagree), (4) Dropdown questions, (5) Open-ended text, (6) Demographic, (7) Ranking questions, (8) Image choice, (9) Click map, (10) File upload, (11) Slider questions, (12) Benchmarkable questions (e.g. Net Promoter Score: 'How likely are you to recommend?').

📐 Extended Central Tendency: Trimmed, Geometric & Harmonic Means

• Trimmed Mean: A robust measure of center obtained by chopping off a fixed percentage (e.g., top and bottom 5%) of extreme values before averaging, preventing outlier distortion.

• Geometric Mean: The $n$-th root of the product of $n$ numbers: $(\prod x_i)^{1/n}$. Ideal for growth rates, ratios, and multiplicative indices.

• Harmonic Mean: The reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of reciprocals: $n / \sum (1/x_i)$. Essential for rates and speeds (e.g., average vehicle speed over fixed distance).

• Empirical Rule of Modes: For unimodal moderately skewed distributions: $\t\text{Mean} - \t\text{Mode} pprox 3(\t\text{Mean} - \t\text{Median})$.

• Choosing Central Tendency: Categorical data $\to$ Mode. Total quantity is of interest $\to$ Mean. Skewed data $\to$ Median.

📏 Extended Dispersion: Quartile Deviation & Coefficient of Variation (CV)

• Quartile Deviation (Semi-Interquartile Range): $\text{QD} = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2}$. Unlike Range, it is robust and unaffected by extreme outliers.

• Coefficient of Variation (CV): $\text{CV} = \frac{\text{Standard Deviation}}{\t\text{Mean}} \times 100\%$. Measures relative dispersion. Slide application: Comparing athlete consistency — higher CV means greater inconsistency.

• Statistical Population vs Sample: Population is described by parameters ($\mu, \sigma$); sample is described by statistics ($ar{x}, s$). Population error: The difference between sample mean and population mean ($ar{x} - \mu$).

• Selecting Index of Dispersion: Bounded variables $\to$ Range. Symmetric distributions $\to$ SD / Variance / CV. Asymmetric / skewed distributions $\to$ Percentiles / IQR.

📈 Quantile Plots, Q-Q Plots & Why Histograms Beat Boxplots

• Quantile Plot: Displays all data sorted in ascending order where each value $x_i$ is plotted against its quantile fraction $f_i = \frac{i - 0.5}{N}$ ($100 f_i\%$ of data $\le x_i$).

• Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) Plot: Graphs the quantiles of one univariate distribution directly against the corresponding quantiles of another. A straight line indicates identical distributions; departures reveal shifts in scale, location, or skewness.

• Histograms vs. Bar Charts: Bar charts plot categorical counts with spaces between bars. Histograms plot continuous quantitative data grouped into bins or intervals.

• Why Histograms Beat Boxplots: Two completely different distributions (e.g., a bimodal distribution with two peaks vs a uniform distribution) can have the exact same 5-number summary (Min, Q1, Median, Q3, Max). A boxplot hides the bimodality, whereas a histogram clearly shows both peaks.

🎨 Advanced Visualizations: Chernoff Faces, Stick Figures & Dimensional Stacking

• Chernoff Faces (1973): Maps up to 18 multidimensional variables to facial features (eye size, eyebrow slant, mouth curve, face eccentricity). Exploits human facial recognition capabilities. Slide example: Visualizing lawyer ratings of 12 judges.

• Stick Figure Visualization: A 5-piece stick icon (1 body + 4 limbs) where two dimensions are mapped to screen coordinates $(x, y)$ and the remaining attributes are mapped to the limb angles and lengths. Dense stick figures form macroscopic texture patterns indicating data clusters and gradients.

• Dimensional Stacking: High-dimensional visualization technique where an $n$-dimensional space is embedded into nested 2D subspaces stacked inside each other. Outer axes hold the most important variables; inner axes hold lower-order variables (practical for up to 9 dimensions).

• Parallel Coordinates: $k$ equidistant parallel vertical axes corresponding to dimensions. Each $k$-dimensional record is drawn as a continuous polygonal line intersecting each vertical axis at its attribute value.

📐 Mathematical Foundations

1. Mean, Variance & Standard Deviation

Mean (μ) = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / n = Σxᵢ / n
Sum all values, divide by count. Sensitive to outliers.
Variance (σ²) = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / n [population] Sample Variance (s²) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1) [sample]
Average of squared distances from mean. Use n−1 for samples (Bessel's correction) to get unbiased estimate.
Standard Deviation (σ) = √Variance = √[Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / n]
Back in original units. About 68% of data falls within μ ± σ for normal distributions.

2. Five Number Summary & IQR

IQR = Q3 − Q1 Outlier if: x < Q1 − 1.5×IQR OR x > Q3 + 1.5×IQR
Q1 = 25th percentile, Q3 = 75th percentile. IQR = interquartile range, robust to outliers.

3. Pearson Correlation Coefficient

r = Σ[(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)] / √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² × Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²]
r ranges from −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive). r = 0 means no linear relationship. Note: r only detects LINEAR relationships — two variables can be perfectly related (e.g., y = x²) with r ≈ 0.

4. Chi-Square Test (for categorical data)

χ² = Σ [(Observed − Expected)² / Expected]
Tests whether two categorical variables are independent. E.g., "Is hair color independent of eye color?" A large χ² value → the variables are likely related.

5. Population vs. Sample

Population (N): The entire set you care about (all voters in India)
Sample (n): A subset you actually measure (1,000 surveyed voters)

Use n−1 (instead of n) for sample statistics to correct for bias (Bessel's correction).
Notation: Population uses μ, σ, N. Samples use x̄, s, n.
