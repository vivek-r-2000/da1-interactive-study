🌍 Why Data Analytics? • Card 1 of 22 Definition
🍳 What is Data?

Analogy first: Imagine you're a chef. You have flour, eggs, sugar, and butter sitting on your counter. By themselves, those raw ingredients tell you nothing — you don't know if they're for a cake, cookies, or pasta.

Now add a recipe (the analysis), and suddenly those ingredients become a chocolate cake (the insight).

Data = raw ingredients. Analytics = the recipe. Insight = the delicious result.

Formally: Data is any raw, unprocessed collection of facts — numbers, text, images, clicks, temperatures. It has no meaning by itself until you process it.

🤔 Why does this matter? Understanding that data ≠ insight prevents the trap of "we have lots of data, so we're fine." Data is only valuable AFTER smart analysis.
💡 Quick Examples of Raw Data:
  • 37.5 — a temperature reading (data)
  • "clicked buy" — a user action (data)
  • 1,200,000 — a sales figure with no context (data)

🌍 Why Data Analytics? • Card 3 of 22 Definition
🕵️ What is Data Mining?

Analogy: Imagine a giant warehouse filled with millions of customer receipts. You can't possibly read them all.

Now imagine hiring a super-smart detective who reads ALL the receipts and tells you: "People who buy diapers on Fridays also tend to buy beer!"

That pattern was completely hidden in the pile. The detective (the algorithm) found it. That's Data Mining.

Formal definition: Data mining is the process of automatically discovering useful patterns, rules, and anomalies from large datasets.

📦
Input
Millions of raw records
⚙️
Process
Smart algorithms
💡
Output
Hidden patterns & rules
🤔 Why does this matter? Companies use data mining to find competitive advantages hidden in their data — patterns no human analyst could spot manually.

⚙️ The KDD Process • Card 8 of 22 Concept
🧹 Data Cleaning

Analogy: Before a chef starts cooking, they wash vegetables, remove rotten pieces, and measure ingredients precisely. You wouldn't cook with muddy, half-rotten carrots!

Data cleaning is the same: before analysis, we must fix or remove dirty data.

🦠 Problems we fix:
  • Missing values (age = ?)
  • Duplicates
  • Noise / errors
  • Inconsistent formats
✅ Solutions:
  • Fill with mean/median
  • Remove duplicates
  • Smooth outliers
  • Standardize formats
🤔 Why it matters: "Garbage in, garbage out." If your data is dirty, even the best algorithm will produce useless results. Cleaning can take 60–80% of project time!
⚙️ The KDD Process • Card 9 of 22 Concept
⛏️ The Mining Step

THIS is where the magic happens. After all the preparation work, we finally unleash the algorithms on the clean, transformed data.

Algorithms hunt for patterns that are:

  • 🔍 Hidden — not obvious to humans
  • 📏 Large-scale — spanning millions of records
  • 🎯 Actionable — you can do something with them

Different algorithms hunt for different things: classification trees, clustering algorithms, association rule miners, neural networks, etc.

Analogy: After all the prep work (cleaning the kitchen, measuring ingredients), you finally turn on the oven. The mining step is putting the cake IN the oven.
⚙️ The KDD Process • Card 10 of 22 Concept
🧐 Pattern Evaluation — Is it INTERESTING?

Not every pattern an algorithm finds is useful!

Example: An algorithm might find that "People who buy salt also buy pepper." That's technically a pattern — but it's obvious and useless for business decisions.

We need patterns that are interesting: novel, unexpected, and actionable.

😴 Boring (trivial):
"People who buy milk also buy cereal" — everyone already knows this.
🤯 Interesting (novel):
"Friday diaper buyers also buy beer" — unexpected! Led to store layout changes.
🤔 Interestingness is measured by: novelty, surprise, usefulness, and statistical significance.

🛠️ Types of Data Mining • Card 14 of 22 Concept
🫧 Clustering — Group Without Labels

Analogy: Imagine sorting your entire wardrobe without anyone telling you the categories. You naturally end up sorting by color, type, or season — because similar items group together.

Clustering does the same with data. No labels needed! It automatically discovers natural groups (clusters) in the data.

Example: A retailer runs clustering on customer purchase history. Without any instructions, the algorithm finds 4 distinct customer types: "bargain hunters", "brand loyalists", "occasional buyers", and "high spenders".

👈 Click the canvas to see clustering in action!

Each color = a different discovered group.

No human told the algorithm there were 3 groups — it found them itself.

Clustering = Unsupervised learning. No labels required. Great for exploration when you don't know what you're looking for yet.
🛠️ Types of Data Mining • Card 15 of 22 Concept
🔗 Association Rules — Beer & Diapers

Association rules find items that frequently appear together. They answer: "People who buy X also tend to buy Y."

The famous story: In the 1990s, Walmart found that men who bought diapers on Friday evenings also tended to buy beer. The insight? Place beer near diapers. Sales of both increased!

Written as a rule: {Diapers, Friday} → {Beer} [confidence: 73%]

🛒 More real-world examples:
  • Bread → Butter (grocery stores)
  • Laptop → Mouse + Laptop Bag (Amazon)
  • Flu shot → Thermometer (pharmacy)
Used everywhere: Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought" is pure association rule mining. So is Netflix's "because you watched..."
🛠️ Types of Data Mining • Card 16 of 22 Concept
📈 Regression — Predict a Number

Analogy: You want to know how much a house will sell for. Based on its size, location, age, and number of bedrooms, can you predict the price?

Regression does exactly this — it predicts a continuous number (not a category). Unlike classification which says "spam or not spam", regression says "this house will cost $425,000."

Input (Features):
  • Size: 1,500 sq ft
  • Location: Downtown
  • Age: 10 years
  • Bedrooms: 3

🛠️ Types of Data Mining • Card 17 of 22 Gotcha
👾 Outlier Analysis — Find the Weirdos!

Analogy: Imagine 1000 people withdrawing $50–$200 from ATMs in their home city. Now someone withdraws $5,000 from Paris, Tokyo, and New York all on the same day. That person stands out — they're an outlier.

Outlier analysis (anomaly detection) finds data points that don't fit the normal pattern. These outliers are often the most interesting data points!

🌐 Applications • Card 21 of 22 Challenges
⚠️ The Hard Parts — Major Challenges

Data mining isn't magic — it comes with real, difficult challenges that researchers and practitioners wrestle with every day.

🌊
Scale

Algorithms must handle billions of rows. An algorithm that takes 1 hour on 1,000 rows might take 1,000 hours on 1 billion rows. Scalability is not optional.

🔒
Privacy

Mining can expose personal patterns. We must ensure data mining doesn't violate individuals' privacy or create discriminatory models.

🎭
Noise & Complexity

Real-world data is messy. Data comes in different formats, with missing values, errors, and irrelevant features that confuse algorithms.

📖 Appendix — First Principles
📖 Appendix: First Principles

Prerequisites explained in plain English. No jargon, no panic.

Simple explanation:

A database is like a giant, highly organised spreadsheet stored on a computer. Instead of one sheet, it has many linked tables. Each table has rows (individual records) and columns (attributes). Example: a hospital database has a "Patients" table with columns like PatientID, Name, Age, Diagnosis.

Why databases matter for data mining:

Data mining almost always reads data FROM databases. Understanding how data is stored helps you understand what data mining can and can't access.

Simple explanation:

SQL (Structured Query Language) is the language you use to "talk" to a database. It's like asking a librarian to find specific books. Example query: SELECT name, age FROM Patients WHERE age > 60 — this retrieves names and ages of all patients older than 60.

Why it's NOT data mining:

A SQL query looks up KNOWN patterns you already specified. Data mining DISCOVERS new patterns you didn't know to look for.

Simple explanation:

An algorithm is a step-by-step recipe for solving a problem. Just like a cooking recipe tells you exactly what to do (step 1: preheat oven; step 2: mix flour...), an algorithm tells a computer exactly what steps to take. Data mining uses algorithms to find patterns — the algorithm is the "detective method."

The critical distinction:

Correlation: Two things happen together (ice cream sales go up when drowning rates go up). This is just a pattern — both are caused by HOT WEATHER.

Causation: One thing actually CAUSES another (smoking causes lung cancer — proven by controlled studies).

Why data mining finds correlations, not causation:

Most data mining algorithms find statistical patterns (correlations). Proving causation requires controlled experiments. Always be careful not to say "X causes Y" just because data mining found them together!

The 5 Categories of Human Mind (Russell Ackoff 1989; Gene Bellinger, Durval Castro, Anthony Mills):

1. Data (Symbols): Raw facts or statements of events without relation to anything else. Simply exists with no inherent meaning. Example: "It is raining" or numbers in a spreadsheet.

2. Information (Data given meaning): Data processed to be useful. Answers who, what, where, and when. Embodies understanding of relations and cause-and-effect. Example: "The temperature dropped 15 degrees and then it started raining." A relational database creates information from stored data.

3. Knowledge (Patterns & Determinism): Application of data and information. Answers how questions. Represents patterns that connect and provide a high level of predictability. Example: "If humidity is very high and temperature drops suddenly, the atmosphere cannot hold moisture, so it rains." Memorizing multiplication tables is amassing knowledge.

4. Understanding (Cognitive & Analytical): Appreciation of why. An interpolative and probabilistic synthesis of new knowledge from prior knowledge (the difference between learning and memorizing). Enables people and AI systems to synthesize answers to questions they haven't seen before.

5. Wisdom (Evaluated Understanding): Systemic awareness of fundamental principles. An extrapolative, non-deterministic, philosophical process calling upon ethical/moral values to discern between right and wrong. Ackoff argued computers will never possess wisdom because it requires human judgment.

Data Characterization (Summarization):

Summarizing the general characteristics or features of a target class of data under study. Example: Summarizing the profile of customers who bought luxury cars last year (e.g., age 40-55, high income, urban residents).

Data Discrimination (Comparison):

Comparing and contrasting the general features of the target class with one or a set of comparative (contrasting) classes. Example: Comparing software buyers who renewed their licenses vs. those who canceled, highlighting the decisive distinguishing features.

The 4 Paradigms of Science (Jim Gray & Alex Szalay 2002):

• Before 1600 (Empirical Science): Describing natural phenomena (observing stars, recording plant growth).

• 1600–1950s (Theoretical Science): Formulating laws and equations (Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations).

• 1950s–1990s (Computational Science): Simulating complex systems (climate models, nuclear reactions).

• 1990–Present (Data Science / The 4th Paradigm): Exploring massive floods of data generated by scientific instruments, sensors, and the Web (terabytes to petabytes). Data mining is the essential driver.

Evolution of Database Systems:

1960s (Primitive File Processing) → 1970s-early 1980s (Relational DBMS & OLTP) → mid-1980s (Advanced Object-Relational & Spatiotemporal DBs) → late-1980s to present (Data Warehousing, OLAP, and Data Mining) → Future Generation Information Systems.

Why Hands-On Practice is Required in Data Analytics:

Edgar Dale's research shows that after 2 weeks, learners tend to remember:

• 10% of what we read (Passive / Verbal)

• 20% of what we hear (Passive / Verbal)

• 30% of what we see (Passive / Visual)

• 50% of what we hear and see (Watching a demonstration / movie)

• 70% of what we say and discuss (Active / Participating in discussions)

• 90% of what we say and do (Active / Simulating real experiences, building models, solving exercises)

Course takeaway: Sincerely doing the lab mini-projects and exercises is essential to internalizing data mining concepts.
